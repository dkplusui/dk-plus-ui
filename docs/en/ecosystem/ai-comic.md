## What an AI Comic Video Is

An AI comic video carries a plot in illustrated frames, combined with narration, dialogue, sound effects, camera movement, and editing to form a continuous viewing experience. AI can assist with the text, image, voice, and video stages, but the quality of the finished work still depends on topic selection, narrative rhythm, character consistency, and human review.

It is not a matter of stitching a few images into a video. It is a complete content production chain: tell the story clearly, unify the characters and the visual style, then use sound and editing to build the emotional rhythm. Each of the six layers owns one job, and a missing layer shows up in the final cut.

| Layer            | What it owns                  | How it fails                                    |
| ---------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Premise          | Whether anyone keeps watching | No hook at the start, slack in the middle       |
| Script           | Conflict and rhythm           | All information ends up in subtitles            |
| Character design | Recognition across episodes   | Faces and outfits change every episode          |
| Storyboard       | Visual narrative              | Flat camera language, blocking is a mess        |
| Sound            | Emotion and space             | Dialogue buried under music, tone jumps         |
| Edit             | Viewing experience            | Pacing drags; information too dense or too thin |

## The Production Chain at a Glance

The full breakdown is on the <a href="/en/ecosystem/ai-comic-workflow">production workflow</a> page. This table is only the skeleton, useful for telling which stage a project is stuck in.

| Stage                           | Key output                                           | Pass condition                                         |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Topic and positioning           | One positioning statement and an audience profile    | You can say in one sentence who has what problem       |
| Script and rhythm               | Scene-level script with information points annotated | Deleting any scene should break the story              |
| Characters and visual standards | Character sheets and a visual standards page         | Three test shots pass the standards                    |
| Storyboard and visuals          | A storyboard table and visual material               | A fixed reviewer spot-checks consistency               |
| Voice and sound                 | Voice tracks and a three-layer sound mix             | On headphones, dialogue stays clear and forward        |
| Editing and publishing          | The finished cut and the publishing version          | Watch it on a small screen, on speaker, and vertically |

There is no reason to enter the next stage before the previous one passes. Rework cost grows toward the end of the chain, and generating visuals before locking character standards is the most expensive mistake.

## Capabilities Worth Accumulating

Do not build your competitiveness on a single model or prompt. Tools change; these production assets do not:

- **Topic library**: validated genres, hook types, and a record of what failed.
- **Templates**: scene script structure, storyboard fields, pacing splits.
- **Character assets**: character sheets, turnaround views, expressions, habitual gestures.
- **Standards**: style keywords, shot annotation format, voice and pacing rules.
- **Checklists**: pre-publish review items, delivery items, retrospective metrics.

These assets survive a model change, and they are what actually compounds.
